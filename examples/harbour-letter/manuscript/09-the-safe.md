# 9. The Safe

They walked down to the office together on Friday without speaking, and that was the compromise: together, and without speaking.

It had taken most of Thursday night to reach. Daniel wanted the letter opened. Sarah wanted it delivered, or at least wanted the man found and asked. Neither would give the other the envelope. In the end, at one in the morning, on the landing, they had agreed on the only thing left, which was that it should go into the office safe, unopened, until Monday, when Mr Aldous would be there to say whose it was in law. Daniel had the safe key. Sarah would watch him turn it. After that it would take the two of them to do anything, and neither trusted the other alone.

It was a miserable thing to have agreed with your own brother. She had not slept.

Marta was already at the long desk. She said good morning to them both and neither quite answered. Sarah saw her take that in, and saw her eyes go to Daniel's face and away again, quickly, the way you look at a dog that has started to growl.

The safe stood in the corner behind the door, a green Milner as old as the firm. Daniel knelt and opened it. Sarah took the envelope out of her coat pocket, where it had been since Wednesday, and for a moment could not make her hand let go.

"Sarah," he said.

She held it out. He did not snatch it. He took it almost gently and, because the light was poor in that corner, he turned towards the window to look at the front of it once more before he put it away.

Marta had stood up to fetch the day book. She was a yard from his shoulder.

Sarah was watching her brother, not Marta, and so she saw it only at the edge of her eye: the older woman stopping as if she had walked into a rope. Marta read the name on the envelope. There was no doubt that she read it; her lips closed on it. Her hand went out and found the edge of the desk, and she stood holding on to the desk and looking at the cream paper in Daniel's hand as though it were a thing she had been told did not exist.

"What is that?" she said. It did not sound like her voice.

Daniel looked round. Something in her face seemed to satisfy him, bitterly.

"It's a letter of my father's. Unposted. It turns out there was one after all." He did not look at Sarah. "My sister has been carrying it since April."

"Since April," Marta said.

"It goes in here until Monday. Nobody opens it. Nobody touches it."

He laid it on the top shelf, on the insurance folder. He shut the safe and turned the key, and put the key in his breast pocket, and stood up. Sarah let out a breath she had been holding since the hill.

"And since we're speaking of Monday," Daniel said. He had turned to face Marta squarely, and his voice had the flat reasonable note Sarah had learned to dread in their father. "The auditors are coming on Monday. Two of them, from the city. They'll want every ledger back to the beginning, and the bank books, and whatever else you have. I'd take it kindly if it was all in order on this desk by nine."

Marta had not taken her eyes from the safe. She brought them to him slowly.

"Auditors," she said.

"I think you know why."

"Dan," Sarah said.

"No. I've been patient. I've asked once and been told to ask no more. So now someone else can ask." He picked up his jacket. "I'm going to the bank. I'll be an hour."

The door closed behind him. The two women stood in the office with the tide clock ticking and the safe between them.

Sarah wanted to say that she did not believe it, that she had told him so. She wanted to ask why a name on an envelope should take the blood out of Marta's face. She did not know how to begin either, and she was afraid of the answer to the second.

"Marta," she said. "Do you know who he is?"

Marta looked at her for a long time. There was something in the look that Sarah could not name, and would think about afterwards: not guilt. It was more like pity.

"Ask me on Monday, child," Marta said, and sat down, and opened the day book, and did not write anything in it.
