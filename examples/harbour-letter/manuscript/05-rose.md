# 5. Rose

Marta Oyelaran did not put the light on. She sat at her own kitchen table in her coat, with the biscuit tin in front of her, and let the room go dark around it.

In the tin were the stubs. She had kept every one. Three hundred and sixty of them, near enough, held in bundles of twelve with rubber bands that had perished and been replaced and perished again. Each was made out in her hand or in Edmund's, and each had the same name on it.

Rose Pike.

Not that anyone had ever said the name in the office. In the ledger it was R.P., and it was Edmund who had chosen that, on a morning thirty years ago when he had shut the door and stood with his back against it and told her there would be a payment on the first of every month to a woman in Calder, and that she was to make it and enter it and never speak of it to a living soul. He had been forty then, with a wife at home and a boy of eight and a girl of four. He had not explained. He had not needed to. Marta had been keeping men's books since she was nineteen and she knew what a payment like that was for.

She had never taken a penny that was not on Edmund's instruction. She would have liked to say so to Daniel this week, in those words. She had seen what he thought. It was in the way he had closed the ledger when she came in.

But it was not hers to say. It had never been hers. Edmund had paid for thirty years and never once said the word son, not to her, not, she was certain, to anyone, and she had made out the stubs and held her tongue and told herself it was loyalty. Rose had died in January. A neighbour had written, a short decent letter, and Marta had shown it to Edmund, and he had read it at the window and said, "Then that's the end of it," and she had made no payment in February. In April he was dead himself.

She had thought, afterwards, that there might be something. A paper. A line in the will. Aldous had read the will out in his dry voice and there had been nothing in it but the two children at home, share and share alike. If Edmund had ever meant to put it right, he had left no word of it. She had grieved for that, privately, more than she had expected to.

And today the boy had walked in out of the wind and asked for his father by name.

She had known him before he opened his mouth. She had never seen him; Edmund had never shown her so much as a photograph. But she had met Rose once, long ago, at a lifeboat dance, a tall quiet girl from up the coast with a wide, serious mouth that turned down a little at one corner. He had Rose's mouth. He had it exactly. And he had Edmund's way of standing, with his weight back on his heels, as though the ground might shift.

*Is there a name I can give the family?* She had asked it and prayed he would not answer, and he had not.

Marta took the newest bundle out of the tin and turned it over. January, in her own hand. She ought to burn them. She had thought so every year. Instead she squared them, as she squared everything, and put them back.

He had gone towards the Ship. He would not stay long; there was nothing to keep him now. In a day or two he would be on the ferry, and Daniel would sell to Hallam, and Sarah would go back to the city, and the stubs would be the only proof left in the world that Edmund Kessler had ever owed anyone anything.

She would say nothing yet. She told herself it was for the children's sake.

She sat on in the dark, an old woman in her coat, and did not believe it.
