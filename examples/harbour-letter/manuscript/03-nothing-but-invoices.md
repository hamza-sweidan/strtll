# 3. Nothing but Invoices

Aldous's letter had come by the first post, and Daniel read it standing up.

It was the usual page and a half. The solicitor regretted the delay; the solicitor awaited the valuation; the solicitor asked, in his last paragraph, as if it had only then occurred to him, whether the family had come across any letters, memoranda or written instructions of the late Mr Kessler's which might bear upon the disposal of the business.

Daniel had not. He had gone through the safe in May and found insurance and a pocket watch. But he had not gone through the desk, because by the time he could bear to open it Sarah had already done it; she had come up the weekend their father was taken ill, and stayed for the funeral, and cleared the desk before she went back, to spare him. He had been grateful. He had not thought to ask what was in it.

She came in at ten with her coat still buttoned, and stood looking at the office as if it were smaller than she remembered. Tom was carrying boxes of old chandlery catalogues out to the skip and had stopped in the doorway to shift his grip.

"Aldous wants to know about Dad's desk," Daniel said. "You cleared it. Was there anything? Letters. Instructions. Anything in his hand that wasn't business."

She did not hesitate, or not so that he saw.

"Nothing but invoices."

"You're sure."

"Invoices and a tide table and about forty pencils. I put the invoices in the box file. You can look."

He believed her. There was no reason not to; she had never been any good at lying, even as a child, and she met his eye when she said it. He wrote *nil* in the margin of Aldous's letter and felt, obscurely, let down. He had wanted there to be something. A line. A reason.

Tom went out with his box. They heard it land in the skip.

"Sit down," Daniel said. "There's something else."

She sat on the edge of the visitor's chair. He had not meant to tell her yet. He had meant to wait for the auditor. But she was here, and she was half of this, and he was tired of carrying it by himself.

"The money I told you about. I know where it went."

"Where?"

"Marta has been robbing us for years."

Her head came up. He went on quickly, before she could speak: the entries, the two initials, the thirty years; the sum on the first of every month in a hand that was more often Marta's than their father's; the way it stopped in January when the old man began to fail. He told her what Marta had said when he asked. Instructions. As if a man like their father gave instructions he did not write down.

"I don't believe that of Marta," Sarah said.

"I didn't want to."

"No, Dan. I mean I don't believe it. She was at Mum's bedside. She taught me long division on that desk."

"And she kept the books, alone, unchecked, for a decade. I'm not saying it for pleasure."

"What did the initials stand for? Did she say?"

"She said to ask her no more." He turned the ledger towards her and put his finger on the line. "R.P. If you can make that into anything honest, tell me, and I'll be glad of it."

She looked at the two letters for longer than they needed. He saw her lips move very slightly, the way they had when she was small and sounding out a word. Then she pushed the ledger back across the desk.

"I can't make it into anything," she said. "But it isn't theft. I'd know."

"How would you know?"

She had no answer to that, or none she gave him. Outside, Tom started the forklift, and the window hummed in its frame.

"Thursday," Daniel said. "Then an auditor. Then we'll see who's right."
