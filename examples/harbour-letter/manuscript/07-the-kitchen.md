# 7. The Kitchen

The valuer had been and gone. He had walked the frontage with a laser measure and a young man's indifference, and named a figure on the doorstep that was lower than Hallam's offer by a margin Daniel could have lived on for five years. So that was settled. He would sell.

He came home wanting to say so, and found his sister sitting at the kitchen table with her coat on and nothing in front of her.

"Sit down, Dan."

He sat. The range was out. She had not started supper.

"I have to tell you something, and I should have told you on Monday, and before Monday. I'd like you to let me get to the end of it."

"All right."

"I have had the letter for months."

He did not understand her. "What letter?"

"Dad wrote a letter. A private one. Not to us." She took her hand out of her coat pocket and laid a long cream envelope on the table between them, and kept two fingers on it. "It's sealed. It's stamped. He never posted it. I have never opened it."

Daniel looked at the envelope. It was his father's stationery; he had a box of it at the office still. It was his father's hand. He turned his head to read the name, since she would not turn the envelope.

*Mr Jonas Pike.*

There was an address under it that he did not take in.

"Who is Jonas Pike?"

"I don't know."

"You've had this for months and you don't know who it's to?"

"I know his name. That's all I know. I've never heard it anywhere else. Not from Dad, not from Mum. I thought you might have."

He had not. He ran through the yard's customers, the creditors, the lifeboat committee, the men his father had fished with as a boy. Nothing. But something moved at the back of his mind, a small cold fish of a thought, and was gone before he could put a hand on it.

"Where did you get it?"

"It doesn't matter where."

"It matters to me."

"I've had it since April. I'm telling you now. That's what I can do."

Since April. He sat back. In April their father had been alive for all but the last day of it. He saw it then, or thought he did: the old man in this kitchen at Easter, with Sarah up for the weekend, taking her aside. Not him. Not the son who had stayed and kept the books and been shouted at across the desk. Her.

"He gave it to you. He trusted you with it."

She did not answer that.

"And told you to keep it from me."

"Nobody told me anything, Dan."

"Then why have you?"

"Because I didn't know what it would do." Her fingers had gone white on the paper. "Because he sealed it and stamped it and then he didn't send it, and I thought, if he couldn't, who was I?"

"If he had wanted it sent, he would have sent it." He heard his own voice, flat and reasonable, his father's voice. "He had every chance in the world to post a letter. So it isn't a letter any more. It's a paper belonging to the estate, and Aldous has been asking for exactly this, and I'm going to open it."

He reached. She drew the envelope back across the table, not far, six inches.

"No."

"Sarah."

"It isn't ours to open. It's got a man's name on it."

"A man neither of us has ever heard of, who may be dead, who may be a debt. There's money gone from that office, a fortune, and Marta's had her hand in it, and now there's a secret letter. Do you not see that those might be one thing?"

She looked at him then with an expression he could not read at all.

"I see it," she said.

"Then give it to me."

"No. Not tonight. Not like this, with you angry." She stood, and put the envelope back in her pocket, and kept her hand over it. "I'll take it to Aldous with you. I'll put it anywhere you like where neither of us can get at it alone. But I won't watch you tear it open at this table."

He let her go up. He sat on in the cold kitchen with the valuer's figure in his head and the name going round and round it. Jonas Pike. Jonas Pike. He was sure he had never heard it. He was less sure, now, that he had never seen it.
