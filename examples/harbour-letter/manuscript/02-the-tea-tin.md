# 2. The Tea Tin

The house smelled as it always had, of coal and damp wool and the sea getting in under the back door.

Sarah Kessler put her suitcase on the kitchen table and listened. Daniel had met her train and dropped her at the gate and gone back to lock up the office; she had perhaps a quarter of an hour. She unzipped the case, folded back the clothes, and worked two fingers under the lining where she had unpicked the stitching in the spring.

The envelope came out warm, as if it had been carried against a body. It was the long cream kind her father had always bought by the box. His handwriting was on the front, square and upright, and a stamp was in the corner, and the flap was sealed. She had never opened it. She had held it to windows and lamps; she had once, in May, got as far as the steam of a kettle, and had turned the kettle off. She did not need to read the name on the front any more. She did not know who he was, the man her father had written to, and she had spent six months not finding out.

She looked round the kitchen for somewhere her brother would never put his hand.

The tea tin was on the top shelf of the dresser where it had been all her life, a red tin with a ship on the lid, and nothing had been kept in it since their mother died. Daniel drank coffee. She stood on a chair and lifted it down. Inside there was a little black dust and the ghost of a smell. She laid the envelope in the tea tin, pressed the lid home, and set it back on its ring of clean wood on the shelf.

When Daniel came in she was peeling potatoes.

He looked tired, and older than thirty-eight. He hung his jacket on the door and sat down in their father's chair without seeming to notice whose it was.

"You've lit the range," he said.

"It was that or freeze."

They ate at the kitchen table. He talked about the valuer who was coming on Thursday and about Mr Aldous the solicitor, who wrote letters at a rate of one a fortnight and charged as if for one a day. She let him talk. It was easier than she had feared, being in the room with him.

Then he put down his fork.

"There is money missing from the books."

She kept her face still. "How much?"

He told her. It was more than the house was worth.

"Over how long?"

"Thirty years. Every month. It stops in January." He rubbed his eyes. "I'm not going to say any more until I've had someone go through it properly. But I wanted you to know before Thursday, because it changes what the place is worth, and it changes what I think about the people in it."

"Dan."

"I mean to sell the office." He said it to the table. "I've meant to since the spring. Hallam's offer lapses on Friday the twenty-third. If we let it go there won't be another, not at that figure, and I am not going to spend the rest of my life keeping a dead man's books for him."

She thought of the red tin eight feet from his shoulder.

"It's half mine," she said. "The office."

"I know that. I'm asking you, not telling you." He looked up at last. "Will you think about it?"

"Yes."

"That's all I want tonight."

He washed the plates and she dried them, as they had at twelve and eight. At the foot of the stairs he stopped with his hand on the newel post.

"It's good you're here," he said, and went up before she could answer.

Sarah stood in the kitchen until she heard his door close. Then she banked the range, and turned out the light, and did not look at the dresser on her way past it.
