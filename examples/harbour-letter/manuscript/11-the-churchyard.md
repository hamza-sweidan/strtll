# 11. The Churchyard

Daniel had come home late on Friday and gone straight up. Whatever Marta had said to him behind the closed door of the office, he had not said it to her; she had heard him walking about his room until two, and at breakfast his place was empty and his car was gone.

So on Saturday morning Sarah did what she had been putting off since Monday, and went to see her father.

The churchyard was on the hill above the harbour, and the wind there was never still. The stone was not up yet. There was a wooden marker with his name on it, and the turf had taken, and somebody, Marta she supposed, had put chrysanthemums in a jar.

There was a man standing at the foot of the grave.

She knew the coat before she knew anything else: grey, good once, turned up at the collar. He was bareheaded as he had been on the quay. He did not hear her on the grass, and she had time to see that he was not praying, or anything like it. He was only standing with his hands in his pockets, looking down, like a man reading a notice.

"Did you know him?" she said.

He turned. Close to, he was younger than she had thought, thirty perhaps, with a wide, serious mouth. And he stood, she noticed, with his weight back on his heels.

"No," he said. "I never met him." He looked at her face, and something in his own altered. "You're his daughter. You were on the quay."

"Sarah Kessler."

"Yes. I thought you must be." He took one hand out of his pocket, and seemed not to know what to do with it, and put it back. "I ought to go. I didn't come here to upset anybody. I only wanted to see where he was."

"Who are you?"

He was silent for so long that she thought he would not say.

"My name is Jonas Pike."

The wind went on blowing. She heard it in the yews. She found that she had known, or that some part of her had, since he lifted his hand from the rail on Wednesday; and that knowing his name told her nothing at all.

"I don't understand," she said. "I'm sorry. I know your name. I don't know who you are."

"No. He wouldn't have told you." He said it without bitterness, as a thing long since worked out. "My mother was Rose Pike. She lived in Calder all her life. She died in January, and it was a slow business, and near the end she said there was something I'd a right to. My mother told me, before she died, that Edmund Kessler was my father."

Sarah put her hand on the wooden marker. It was the nearest thing.

"She said he'd paid for me. Every month, never late. She said he was a decent man who'd done one wrong thing and I wasn't to hate him. I didn't hate him. I didn't know what I thought. It took me till October to find out that what I thought was that I wanted to look at him once." He glanced down at the turf. "I came to the office on Tuesday. A woman told me he had died. April, she said. I'd been making up my mind for nine months and I was six months late."

Rose Pike. Every month. R.P., she thought, and saw Daniel's finger on the line, and Marta's face at the safe. Not theft. She had been right about that, and it was no comfort whatever.

He had her father's hands. She saw it now that she was looking.

"Mr Pike," she said, and stopped, and began again. "Jonas. He wrote to you."

He stared at her.

"My father left a letter for you. It was never posted. It has your name on it and your address in Calder, in his writing, with a stamp. I don't know what it says. Nobody has opened it." She made herself go on. "I've had it since he died. I should have sent it. I didn't know who you were, and I was afraid of it, and I kept it. I'm telling you because you ought to hear that from me and not from a solicitor."

"A letter," he said.

"It's in the safe at the office. My brother has the key. He doesn't know you're here; he doesn't know you exist, unless..." She thought of the closed door, the footsteps till two. "I don't know what he knows. Come on Monday. Nine o'clock. I'll bring you in myself."

He looked at the marker, and then at her, and she saw that he was trying, and failing, to keep his face in order.

"He wrote to me," Jonas Pike said.

"Yes."

"And stamped it."

"Yes," Sarah said. "He stamped it."
