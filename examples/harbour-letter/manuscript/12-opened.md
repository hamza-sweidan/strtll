# 12. Opened

He had expected Aldous. The solicitor had telephoned at half past eight to say that his train was delayed, and Daniel had put down the receiver with something like relief, and gone on doing what he had been doing since seven, which was not looking at the safe.

He had not told Sarah. All weekend he had meant to. He had stood outside her door on Saturday night with the words ready, *we have a brother*, and had not been able to make his hand knock; and on Sunday she had kept out of his way so thoroughly that he had wondered what she was avoiding. He had put the auditors off. He had said good morning to Marta and she had said it back. That was as far as any of it had got.

So he was not ready when the door opened and his sister came in out of the wind with a stranger behind her.

A man of thirty, bareheaded, in a grey coat. He stopped just inside the door, and looked, not at Daniel, but at the photograph of the lifeboat crew on the wall.

Tom, coming through from the yard with the post, stopped too.

"That's the fellow who wanted the ferry times," he said, to nobody in particular, and then looked at Marta, and went red.

Marta had risen from her chair. She did not look at Tom. She was looking at the stranger as she had looked at the envelope on Friday, and Daniel, seeing her face, understood before his sister spoke.

"Dan," Sarah said. She was very pale. "This is Jonas Pike. He is our father's son. I met him on Saturday, at the grave. I didn't know how to tell you. I didn't know if you..."

"I know," Daniel said. "Marta told me. Friday."

They looked at each other, he and Sarah, across the width of the office, each with a weekend's silence to account for. It was almost funny. He found he could not look at the other man yet, and looked at his shoes instead, which were wet, and had been good.

"Mr Kessler," Jonas Pike said. A quiet voice, from further north. "I'm not here to ask for anything. Your sister said there was a letter. I'd like to read it, and then I'll go."

"You'd better sit down," Daniel said. It was not gracious, but it was what came.

There was Tom, still in the doorway with his mouth open and the post in his hand. Daniel sent Tom out to the yard and shut the door. Then he took the key from his breast pocket, and knelt, and opened the safe.

The envelope was where he had put it. He had wanted, on Thursday, to tear it open with his thumb. He carried it across the room now in both hands, like something that might spill, and held it out, and Jonas Pike took it.

He did not open it at once. He read the front: his own name, his mother's street. He touched the stamp. Then he slid a finger under the flap, and the old gum gave with a small dry sound, and he drew out two sheets and unfolded them and began, without being asked, to read aloud. Perhaps he thought he owed them that. His voice was not quite steady.

It was not long. It began *Dear Jonas*, and said that the writer had no right to begin so. It said that he had known of him for thirty years and seen him once, at a distance, at a school gate in Calder, and had driven home. It said he was sorry, plainly, twice. And then:

"I acknowledge you as my son, and I give you a half share in the harbour office. It is little enough, and late. Daniel will not thank me, and he will be right, but it was his grandfather's before it was mine and it should have been yours as much as theirs."

Nobody spoke. Daniel heard the tide clock. A half share. He thought of Hallam, and Friday, and a signature that would now need a third hand, and found that none of it would come into focus.

Jonas turned the page over. There was nothing on the back.

"It isn't dated," he said.

"He stamped it," Marta said. "He stamped it. He meant it to go."

"He never posted it," said Sarah. Her eyes were on the floor. "It was sealed and stamped and he never posted it. Nobody will ever know what he meant."

Daniel looked at her. He trusted her with it, he thought, and not me; and even now it had a sting. Then he looked, at last, at his brother, who was folding the letter along its creases with their father's hands.
