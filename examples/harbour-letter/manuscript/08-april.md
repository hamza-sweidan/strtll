# 8. April

*Six months earlier*

The harbour office at night was a place Sarah had not been in since she was a child, and it was smaller in the dark, and it smelled of her father.

She had come for his reading glasses. That was the errand the ward sister had given her at nine o'clock, kindly, to get her out of the corridor: his glasses, and the folder with the insurance in it, and a pair of pyjamas if there were any. He had gone down in this room at four in the afternoon with a pencil in his hand. Daniel had ridden in the ambulance. Daniel was at the hospital now, asleep in a plastic chair, and she had taken the keys out of his jacket without waking him.

She put on the desk lamp and not the big light.

The glasses were on the blotter where they had fallen. The folder was in the safe, and she found it. Then, because she did not want to go back yet, because the corridor was waiting with its vending machine and its clock, she sat down in his chair and pulled open the desk drawer.

Invoices. A tide table. More pencils than one man could need. She lifted the invoices out in a block to square them, the way Marta would have, and under them, flat against the wood, was an envelope.

It was one of his own long cream ones. It was sealed. There was a stamp on it, first class, stuck on straight. And it was addressed, in the square upright hand that had written her name in every birthday card of her life:

*Mr Jonas Pike, 14 Tannery Row, Calder.*

She read it twice. She did not know who Jonas Pike was. She had never heard the name; not at this desk, not at home, not in any of the long careful telephone calls that were all she and her father had managed these last years. It was not a business letter. He typed those, or Marta did. This was his own hand and his own stamp, and it had been put where a man puts a thing he does not want found and cannot throw away.

How long had it been there? There was no postmark to say. The invoices on top of it were from the last fortnight. She knew, because Daniel had told her on the telephone, that he and their father had quarrelled badly that last week, about selling, the worst they had ever had. She did not know whether he had written it before that quarrel or after. It seemed to matter, and she could not have said why.

She turned it over. Nothing on the back. She held it up to the lamp and saw only the shadow of folded paper, two sheets, perhaps three.

Whether he had meant to send it and run out of days, or had decided against it and could not bring himself to burn it, the envelope did not say.

Her telephone rang in her bag.

It was loud in the small room, and she knew before she had it in her hand. She knew by the hour. It was ten past eleven. The ward sister's voice was the same kind voice, slowed down.

"Miss Kessler. I'm so sorry. I'm afraid your father died a few minutes ago. Your brother is with him. It was very peaceful."

She said thank you. She said she would come. She put the telephone down on the blotter beside the glasses he would not now need, and sat without moving while the tide clock on the wall went on telling the hour of high water to nobody.

Daniel would be standing in that corridor by himself.

She ought to have put the envelope back. She thought so afterwards, often. It would have been the work of a second to lay it under the invoices and close the drawer and let whoever cleared the desk find it, in daylight, with the family round them. But she saw Daniel finding it. She saw him, tonight of all nights or next week, with their father's last quarrel still in his ears, reading a stranger's name in that hand.

She put the envelope inside her coat.

Then she picked up the glasses and the folder, and turned off the lamp, and locked the door behind her with her brother's keys.
