# 1. The Valuation

The valuer was coming on Thursday, and Daniel Kessler had given himself until then to understand his father's books.

He had the ledgers out across the long desk in the harbour office, thirty years of them, green cloth going grey at the corners. His father had died in April. It was October now, and the estate was still not settled, and Hallam Marine had put an offer on the table that lapsed on Friday the twenty-third. Daniel meant to take it. He had meant to take it since the last week of April, when he and his father had shouted at each other across this same desk about selling, and nothing since had changed his mind.

Outside, the tide was making. He could hear Tom Brandt in the yard, dragging the chain locker across the cobbles.

It was the third ledger that stopped him. Halfway down a page in his father's square hand was an entry with no supplier against it, only two initials. R.P. He turned back a month and found it again. And the month before. He went on turning. The same sum, or near it, on or about the first of every month, year after year, climbing a little with the decades the way a wage climbs. He pulled the earliest ledger towards him and it was there too, on the second page. Thirty years of it. The last entry was in January. After January there was nothing.

He did the sum on the back of an envelope and sat looking at the figure.

Marta Oyelaran came in at nine, as she had come in at nine since before he could remember, and hung her coat on the hook that was hers. She had kept these books beside his father for most of those thirty years. In the last ten the entries were in her hand more often than his.

"Marta," he said. "What is R.P.?"

She did not pretend not to hear. She stood with one hand still on her coat, and he watched her decide how much to say.

"Those were your father's instructions."

"Instructions to pay whom?"

"Ask me no more than that, Daniel."

"It's a great deal of money."

"I know what it is. I wrote most of it down."

"And it stopped in January."

"Yes," she said. "It stopped in January." She took her hand from the coat and went through to the back room, and he heard the kettle go on.

He did not believe her. He sat with his father's ledgers open in front of him and tried to, and could not. His father had been a careful man, close with money, a man who queried the price of rope. Such a man did not pay out a sum like that every month for thirty years and leave no name against it. But a bookkeeper might. A bookkeeper who wrote the entries herself, who was trusted, who was never checked, might take a little every month and set two letters beside it and call it instructions when at last somebody asked.

Marta had been taking money for years. The columns said so; there was no other way to read them. And she had stopped in January because his father was failing by then and she had been afraid of who would look at the books when he was gone.

He closed the ledger. Through the window he could see Tom coiling the chain, whistling, twenty-two and in nobody's confidence. Sarah's train got in at six. He would tell her tonight that there was money missing, and by how much, and he would not yet say who. He wanted the valuer's figure first, and then an auditor's. He wanted it in writing before he said the name aloud.

From the back room came the small sound of a cup set down on its saucer. Marta did not bring him one. In all the years he had worked there she had never once failed to bring him one.

He drew the envelope with his arithmetic on it towards him, folded it in half, and put it in his breast pocket where his father had always kept the tide tables.
