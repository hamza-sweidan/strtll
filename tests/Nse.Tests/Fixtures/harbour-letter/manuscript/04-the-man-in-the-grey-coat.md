# 4. The Man in the Grey Coat

Tom Brandt saw him first, because Tom was on the forklift and could see over the wall.

A man in a grey coat was standing on the far side of the road with his back to the water, looking at the harbour office the way you look at a house you are thinking of buying, or one you used to live in. He was not from the town. Tom knew everyone in the town, at least by their dogs. The man was perhaps thirty, bareheaded, with a canvas bag over one shoulder, and he stood there through two loads of pallets before he crossed.

Tom cut the engine and followed him in. It was not nosiness. Mr Daniel and his sister had gone up to the bank, and Marta was by herself.

The man had stopped just inside the door. Marta was at the long desk with the day book, and she had not yet looked up.

"Good afternoon," the man said. "I'm sorry to walk in. I'm looking for Mr Edmund Kessler."

Marta looked up then.

Tom had worked in that office for four years, and he had seen Marta face down a harbourmaster, two tax inspectors and a drunk trawler skipper with a boathook. He had never seen her do what she did now, which was nothing at all. She sat with the pen in her hand and her mouth a little open. The colour went out of her face so completely that he took a step towards her.

"Mr Kessler died in April," she said.

The man did not move. Then he put out a hand and found the door frame with it.

"April," he said.

"The thirtieth. It was sudden. I'm sorry; you weren't to know."

"No." He had a quiet voice, not local, from somewhere further north. "No, I wasn't to know. I should have written first. I thought if I wrote he might not..." He stopped. He seemed to see Tom for the first time, and then the ledgers, and the tide clock, and the photograph of the old man with the lifeboat crew that hung behind the desk. He looked at the photograph for a long moment.

"Is there anything I can do for you?" Marta said. Her voice was very careful. "Is there a message? A name I can give the family?"

"No," the man said. "No, there's no message. Thank you. I'm sorry to have troubled you."

He went out. Tom watched through the window as he crossed the road again, not looking back, and walked away along the quay towards the Ship Hotel with his bag knocking against his hip.

"Who was that?" Tom said.

Marta had put the pen down. She was squaring the day book against the edge of the desk, exactly, as if it mattered.

"A traveller. He wanted the ferry times."

"He asked for Mr Kessler."

"He asked for the office. People do. They see the name over the door." She stood up and she was steadier, but she kept one hand on the chair. "Have you done the pallets?"

"Two more."

"Then do the two more, Tom. And there's no call to go bothering Mr Daniel with every stranger who comes in out of the wind. He has enough to think about this week."

Tom went back out to the forklift. He was not a suspicious young man and he liked Marta, who had lent him the deposit on his room and never mentioned it since. A traveller, then. It was possible. The ferry did go from the north pier, and people did get lost.

But he sat for a minute before he started the engine, looking along the empty quay. Tom had no idea who he was. He only knew that the man had asked for a dead man by his full name, and that Marta, who was afraid of nothing, had looked at him as though he had come up through the floor.
