# 6. Low Water

On Wednesday Daniel went to the office at seven, and at eight Sarah stood on the kitchen chair and lifted down the red tin.

She had decided in the night. It had seemed simple at three in the morning, as things do: the envelope had a stamp on it and an address on it, and there was a post office on Fore Street with a brass slot in the wall. She would do what the envelope asked. She would put it in the slot and walk away, and it would stop being hers.

She put it in her coat pocket and kept her hand on it all the way down the hill.

The tide was out. The harbour at low water was a different place, all mud and green chain and boats lying over on their sides like sleepers. She walked the length of Fore Street and stopped across from the post office and stood there, a woman in a good coat, looking at a slot in a wall.

He had stamped it. That was what she could never get past. A man does not stamp a letter he has decided not to send. But he had not sent it, either; he had kept it by him with the stamp on it for a day, or a week, or a month, there was no telling, and he had walked past pillar boxes every day of his life. He had decided against it; she had told herself so for months. It was the only reading that let her off. If he had changed his mind, then keeping it was a kind of obedience, and she was a dutiful daughter with a dead man's second thoughts in her pocket. If he had not changed his mind, she was something else.

And there was Daniel's ledger now. The second initial in Daniel's ledgers was the first letter of the surname on the envelope. She had noticed it on Tuesday, with his finger on the line, and had tried not to. It might be nothing. Half the surnames on this coast began with it. But thirty years of money had gone somewhere, and her father had written one private letter that she knew of in his life, and she did not think she believed in that much coincidence.

If she posted it, Daniel would never know there had been anything to know. Marta would go on being blamed.

She did not post it.

She walked instead out along the north quay, where the wind was, as far as the ferry steps, and stood with her back to the bollard and her hand still in her pocket. From there you could see the whole front: the Ship, the chandler's, the lifeboat shed, the harbour office with KESSLER over the door in letters her grandfather had paid for.

There was a man looking at it.

He was on the quay below the Ship, perhaps eighty yards from her, leaning on the rail. A man in a grey coat, bareheaded, with the wind pulling at his hair. He was not looking at the boats, or the view. He was looking at the office, steadily, the way she herself had been looking at the slot in the post office wall, as at a thing he had come a long way to do and could not now begin.

She had no idea who he was. A buyer, perhaps; one of Hallam's people, sizing up the frontage before the valuer did. She felt a small cold dislike of him on Daniel's behalf, and then on her own.

He must have felt himself watched. He turned his head, and for a moment the two of them regarded each other along the length of the empty quay. She could not see his face clearly. He lifted one hand a few inches from the rail, not a wave, hardly a greeting, and let it fall again.

Then he pushed himself upright and went in at the door of the Ship.

Sarah waited until her fingers were too cold to feel the paper. Then she walked home the long way, by the churchyard, with her father's letter in her coat.
