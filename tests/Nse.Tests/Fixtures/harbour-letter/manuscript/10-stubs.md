# 10. Stubs

When Daniel came back from the bank, Sarah had gone, and Marta was sitting at the long desk with a biscuit tin in front of her.

It was an old Huntley and Palmers tin with the paint worn off the corners. He had never seen it before. She had both hands flat on the lid.

"Shut the door, Daniel."

"If this is about Monday..."

"It's about thirty years. Shut the door. Your father shut it, the day he told me, and stood with his back to it. You may as well do the same."

He shut the door. He did not stand with his back to it; he came and stood over her, and she took her hands off the lid and opened the tin and turned it round to face him.

Paper. Bundles of narrow paper slips in rubber bands, dozens of bundles, packed on their edges like a card index. She lifted one out and put it in his hand.

They were counterfoils. Stubs, from a money-order book, the old kind the post office used to issue. Each had a date, and a sum, and a payee. He knew the sums. He had been staring at them for a week. He turned over a dozen, two dozen, with his thumb.

The payee was the same on every one.

"R.P. is Rose Pike. Your father paid her every month for thirty years. I made the payments out, because he asked me to, and I wrote them in the ledger the way he told me to write them, and I kept the stubs because somebody in this office had to be able to prove where the money went." Her voice was quite steady. "There they are. Three hundred and fifty-seven. Count them against the ledger. You won't find one short."

He was still turning them over. Calder. A post office in Calder, month after month.

"Who is Rose Pike?"

"Who was she. Rose died in January. That's why it stops."

"Who was she, then?"

"She was a girl from up the coast that your father knew for one summer, when you were seven, and your mother was ill that whole year." Marta did not look away from him. "She had a child the next spring. A boy."

The slips were very light in his hand. He put them down on the desk because he was afraid of dropping them.

"No."

"Jonas is your father's son."

"No. He'd have... He wasn't a man who..."

"He was exactly a man who. He was a man who did one thing in his life he was ashamed of and then paid for it on the first of the month for thirty years and never missed. That's who he was. I'm not asking you to like it. I didn't."

*Mr Jonas Pike.* The cream envelope. His father's square hand. The small cold thought that had slipped away from him in the kitchen on Thursday came back and lay still, and he looked at it. Pike. R.P.

"You knew his name this morning," he said. "When you saw the envelope."

"I knew his name the day he was christened. Your father told me, standing at that window. He never spoke of him again. He never saw him, so far as I know. He never wrote. I'd have sworn to that, until this morning." She touched the lid of the tin. "I thought he'd left nothing. I thought he'd gone to his grave pretending. I was angry with him for it, God forgive me, all this summer."

Daniel sat down. He found that he had sat in the visitor's chair, on the wrong side of his own desk.

She had not taken a penny. That came to him slowly, the way feeling comes back to a hand. Thirty years, alone, unchecked, with a secret that would have paid any price she named, and she had made out the slips and kept the proof and let him look at her on Monday as he had looked.

"The auditors," he said.

"Let them come. I've nothing to fear from arithmetic."

"I'll ring them. I'll put them off." He could not look up. "Marta. I thought..."

"I know what you thought. You closed the ledger when I walked in." She began to fit the bundle back into its place among the others. "You're his son too. You jump the same way."

He let that go; he had earned it.

"Does Sarah know? Any of it?"

"Not from me."

"The letter. What's in it?"

"I don't know. I didn't know there was a letter. But I know who it belongs to, Daniel, and so do you, now." She closed the tin. "What you do about that is between you and your sister. I've carried my share."
